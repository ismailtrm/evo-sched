\begin{abstract}
Kubernetes node placement couples scheduling delay, resource balance, and workload consolidation. We present a live custom scheduler whose scoring policy is represented by four nonnegative, normalized weights: \texttt{FreeCPU}, \texttt{FreeMem}, \texttt{Balance}, and \texttt{Consolidation}. Candidate policies are evaluated by patching a running scheduler deployment, replaying 24-Pod synthetic workloads, and measuring high-resolution scheduling-decision latency and maximum workload-occupied worker count. An NSGA-II-style search evaluated 216 candidates across three seeds on a 4-worker kind cluster. Although each single-evaluation search produced apparently novel Pareto points, none of five finalists remained nondominated under repeated-mean evaluation using a ten-repeat validation protocol pre-specified and committed before execution. This negative result shows that live-cluster timing noise can mislead expensive black-box search when each candidate is measured once. An independent ten-repeat holdout nevertheless produced a stable three-point baseline tradeoff: spread-heavy, balanced, and consolidation-heavy policies occupied four, three, and two workers, with mean p95 decision latencies of $1.253$, $1.260$, and $1.304$ s, respectively. The latency confidence intervals overlap, so the ordering is descriptive rather than a significance claim. The study contributes a reproducible live-cluster search method, an identifiable policy representation, and evidence that repeated validation is essential when optimizing scheduler measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Kubernetes scheduling chooses a feasible node for each pending Pod and therefore influences placement delay, multi-resource fragmentation, and workload consolidation. Production cluster managers such as Borg combine placement, packing, admission, and isolation policies to improve utilization \cite{ref-borg}. Kubernetes exposes a pluggable scheduling cycle that filters invalid nodes before scoring the feasible set \cite{ref-k8s-framework}. This separation makes the scoring policy a natural optimization surface, but it does not remove the central systems difficulty: a candidate policy must be evaluated against a live, variable control plane.

This paper studies that difficulty on a reproducible Kubernetes deployment. We expose a compact four-weight scoring policy, connect it to an NSGA-II-style optimizer, and evaluate candidates by executing real Pod creation and binding operations. The two objectives are p95 scheduling-decision latency and maximum workload-occupied worker count. The latter is a consolidation proxy, not a count of powered, autoscaled, or billable infrastructure nodes.

The research question is deliberately falsifiable: \emph{does single-evaluation evolutionary search find tradeoff points that remain Pareto-relevant under repeated live-cluster validation?} The answer in our experiments is no. Three seeds produce promising one-shot candidates, but all five finalists are dominated when re-evaluated ten times on each training workload. This changes the contribution from a claim of optimizer superiority to a methodological result about noisy live fitness. Separately, an unseen mixed-burst workload confirms that the parameterized baselines express a genuine latency--consolidation tradeoff.

The main contributions of the paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item A real custom scheduler and evaluator that perform feasibility filtering, Pod binding, deployment reconfiguration, workload replay, cleanup, and provenance capture on a live Kubernetes cluster.
\item A versioned four-feature policy whose nonnegative weights are normalized to remove positive-scale equivalence and whose signals independently affect representative node rankings.
\item An official three-seed search and repeated-validation protocol, pre-specified and committed before execution, covering 16,200 search Pods and 5,760 validation Pods, with the default Kubernetes scheduler retained as an external placement reference.
\item An empirical result showing both a held-out three-point baseline tradeoff and the failure of one-shot optimizer improvements to survive repeated evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Problem Statement}
Kubernetes scheduling can be viewed as a filtering and ranking process. The scheduling cycle selects a node, and the binding cycle applies that decision \cite{ref-k8s-framework}. Feasibility is not optional bookkeeping: a node that is not \texttt{Ready}, is explicitly unschedulable, carries an untolerated taint, or lacks requested CPU or memory must not be scored as a valid target \cite{ref-k8s-node-status,ref-k8s-taints}. Optimization therefore begins only after the feasible set $\mathcal{F}$ is established.

This distinction matters for the present work. We do not optimize over arbitrary nodes in the cluster; we optimize how to rank nodes that already satisfy operational constraints. Concretely, the custom scheduler implemented in this paper filters candidate nodes according to readiness, the \texttt{unschedulable} flag, taint--toleration compatibility, and resource fit. Only the surviving nodes are scored by the parameterized policy. As a result, the search problem is not ``which invalid placements should be allowed,'' but ``how should a valid candidate set be prioritized under competing objectives.''

The optimization target is the scoring policy. Let $\mathbf{w}$ denote its four weights. For any fixed vector, the scheduler is deployed, exercised on the three training workloads, and measured using two objectives:
\begin{equation}
\min \; f_1(\mathbf{w}) = \mathrm{AvgP95DecisionLatency}(\mathbf{w})
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\min \; f_2(\mathbf{w}) = \mathrm{AvgMaxOccupiedWorkers}(\mathbf{w})
\end{equation}
where each objective is aggregated across the workload suite. The first captures tail delay from evaluator submission to the custom scheduler's node decision. The second is the maximum number of workers simultaneously hosting non-terminal evaluator Pods and acts only as a consolidation proxy.

The objectives are not collapsed into a scalar fitness. Pareto analysis preserves the exchange between delay and consolidation instead of selecting an arbitrary cost coefficient. We make no claim that a policy is universally best or that the custom scheduler dominates the Kubernetes reference scheduler.

\section{Related Work}
Large-scale cluster schedulers expose several ways to trade placement quality, responsiveness, and utilization. Borg uses admission control, task packing, over-commitment, and machine sharing in production clusters \cite{ref-borg}; Omega studies shared-state parallel scheduling under optimistic concurrency \cite{ref-omega}; and Firmament uses min-cost max-flow to obtain high-quality centralized placements at scale \cite{ref-firmament}. Multi-resource work such as Dominant Resource Fairness formalizes allocation across heterogeneous resource demands \cite{ref-drf}, while Tetris explicitly packs tasks using multiple resource dimensions to reduce fragmentation \cite{ref-tetris}. Our contribution is narrower: we do not propose a production-scale scheduler architecture or fairness mechanism, but expose and experimentally search a small policy inside a live Kubernetes control loop.

Kubernetes itself provides configurable profiles and score plugins, including resource-fit, topology, affinity, and taint-related plugins \cite{ref-k8s-config}. The platform also permits multiple named schedulers in one cluster. We use that mechanism to run a separate custom scheduler and retain the unmodified \texttt{default-scheduler} profile as an external placement reference.

Multi-objective Kubernetes scheduling is itself established. Di Cicco et al. formulate offline scheduling and replica allocation across heterogeneous Kubernetes clusters as a multi-objective integer program, then apply NSGA-II, NSGA-III, and multi-swarm particle swarm optimization to latency, cost, and interruption objectives \cite{ref-dicicco}. Gouaouri et al. integrate TOPSIS and centralized and distributed NSGA-II into an online, power-aware Kubernetes scheduler for power consumption, resource utilization, and load balancing \cite{ref-gouaouri}. Existing work primarily asks whether multi-objective or evolutionary scheduling can improve placement objectives. This study instead examines whether apparent improvements remain valid when fitness is obtained from noisy, repeated live-cluster measurements.

NSGA-II supplies the nondominated sorting, crowding, and elitist selection used in our optimizer \cite{ref-nsga2}. However, live systems create uncertain fitness through timing jitter and environmental variation. Prior evolutionary-computation literature identifies re-evaluation and other uncertainty-handling measures as necessary when fitness is noisy \cite{ref-noisy-ea}. More recent analysis shows that inaccurate multi-objective estimates can cause selection errors that conventional performance metrics fail to represent \cite{ref-noisy-moo-metrics}. Our experiment gives a concrete Kubernetes instance of that warning: apparent one-shot improvements disappear under repeated validation.

\section{System Design}
The platform is a kind cluster with one control-plane node and four homogeneous workers \cite{ref-kind}. It preserves the Kubernetes API server, scheduler coexistence, deployment rollout, and Pod binding path while remaining reproducible on one host. Official manifests record the clean Git commit, annotated protocol tag, workload and deployment checksums, cluster topology, server and client versions, scheduler image identity, preflight state, and postflight restoration.

The custom scheduler runs as a single-replica deployment in namespace \texttt{evo-sched}. RBAC permits it to list Pods and Nodes and create Pod bindings. It polls unbound Pods whose \texttt{schedulerName} is \texttt{evok8s-scheduler}, builds node state from allocatable resources and assigned Pod requests, filters infeasible nodes, scores the survivors, and submits a Kubernetes \texttt{Binding}. The deployment uses a \texttt{Recreate} strategy because overlapping scheduler replicas caused a binding race during a pilot rollout; official runs require exact one-replica convergence before replay begins.

The evaluator operates in namespace \texttt{evo-sched-eval}. For each candidate it patches environment-based weights, blocks until rollout convergence, creates Pods according to a versioned workload JSON, collects timings and requested-resource samples, removes managed Pods, and restores the original policy. Official runs also restore the normal two-second idle cadence after experiments. During measurement, both the custom scheduling loop and evaluator sampling interval are 250 ms. Thus, orchestration remains fixed while only the policy vector and workload vary.

\section{Parameterized Scheduling Policy}
The V1 pilot used an unnormalized representation containing a packing term that was linearly dependent on free CPU and memory for homogeneous workers. Positive scaling also preserved node rankings. V2 removes both ambiguities: weights are nonnegative and normalized onto the L1 simplex,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}=(w_c,w_m,w_b,w_k),\quad w_i\geq0,\quad\sum_i w_i=1.
\end{equation}
This canonical form prevents scale-equivalent chromosomes from being evaluated as distinct policies.

For a feasible node $s$ and Pod request $(r_c,r_m)$, post-placement free fractions are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
q_c(s)&=\frac{\max(0,\mathrm{FreeCPU}(s)-r_c)}{\mathrm{AllocCPU}(s)},\\
q_m(s)&=\frac{\max(0,\mathrm{FreeMem}(s)-r_m)}{\mathrm{AllocMem}(s)}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The balance feature rewards complementary placements,
\begin{equation}
 B(s)=1-|q_c(s)-q_m(s)|,
\end{equation}
and the consolidation feature is an occupancy indicator,
\begin{equation}
 K(s)=\mathbb{I}[\text{$s$ already hosts a non-terminal evaluator Pod}].
\end{equation}
The final score is
\begin{equation}
S(s)=w_cq_c(s)+w_mq_m(s)+w_bB(s)+w_kK(s).
\end{equation}
Free-capacity weights favor spreading, $B$ favors CPU--memory balance, and $K$ favors already occupied workers. Tests establish that each feature changes at least one representative node ranking independently. The highest-scoring feasible node is chosen, with node name as a deterministic tie-breaker.

\section{Evolutionary Policy Search}
Each individual is one normalized V2 weight vector. Evaluation deploys it and averages outcomes across \texttt{cpu-tight-train}, \texttt{mem-tight-train}, and \texttt{fragmenting-mix-train}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min f_1(\mathbf{w})&=\mathrm{AvgP95DecisionLatency}(\mathbf{w}),\\
\min f_2(\mathbf{w})&=\mathrm{AvgMaxOccupiedWorkers}(\mathbf{w}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The optimizer uses population 12, five offspring generations, uniform crossover, and per-gene Gaussian mutation with probability $0.8$ and standard deviation $0.25$. Raw genes are clamped to $[0,2]$ and normalized before evaluation. NSGA-II-style nondominated sorting, crowding distance, tournament selection, and elitist replacement preserve multiple objective regimes \cite{ref-nsga2}. The three manual baselines are measured in each seed but are reference points, not reproductive individuals.

Seeds 42, 123, and 999 each evaluate 72 optimizer candidates plus three baselines. Candidate fitness is intentionally one live execution per training workload because evaluation is expensive; the consequence of that choice is tested explicitly by selecting all five single-evaluation Pareto finalists and re-evaluating them with the three baselines under a shared ten-repeat protocol. Hyperparameter sensitivity is outside scope.

\section{Evaluation Methodology and Experimental Setup}
\textit{Workloads.} Each version-controlled workload is a list of Pod arrival times, CPU and memory fractions, and durations. Fractions are resolved against the minimum worker allocatable capacity, which was 16,000 millicores and 31,712 MiB. Every workload contains 24 ten-second Pods arriving in two 12-Pod bursts at $t=0$ and $t=16$ s. \texttt{cpu-tight-train} and \texttt{mem-tight-train} use $(0.22,0.06)$ and $(0.06,0.22)$ CPU--memory fractions per Pod. \texttt{fragmenting-mix-train} alternates $(0.32,0.08)$ and $(0.08,0.32)$. The unseen \texttt{mixed-burst-holdout} instead combines $(0.23,0.08)$, $(0.08,0.23)$, and $(0.14,0.14)$ Pods.

\textit{Measurements.} The evaluator records a nanosecond-resolution submission timestamp immediately before the Pod create request. The custom scheduler records its decision immediately before the bind request. Their difference is \emph{scheduling-decision latency}. Kubernetes \texttt{PodScheduled} condition time and the evaluator's first observation of \texttt{spec.nodeName} are retained separately; they are not used as the primary objective. The default kube-scheduler cannot emit our decision annotation, so its decision latency is unavailable rather than imputed from a coarser source.

Every 250 ms, the evaluator counts workers hosting non-terminal managed Pods and computes requested-load support metrics from Pod requests divided by run-start allocatable capacity. Dominant requested load on a worker is the larger CPU or memory requested fraction; the reported load variance is the population variance of that value across the four workers, averaged over samples. It is a placement-balance measure, not hardware utilization. Completion and unschedulable counts are safety checks.

\textit{Protocols.} Official baselines use three repetitions for each of three custom policies and the default kube-scheduler on each training workload. The evolutionary stage evaluates each candidate once per training workload for three seeds. Repeated validation then evaluates five optimizer finalists and all three baselines ten times per training workload. Finally, a selection rule pre-specified and committed before execution carries repeated-front representatives plus baselines to the holdout; because no optimizer finalist remains nondominated, deduplication leaves the three baselines. Latency uncertainty is summarized with sample standard deviation, extrema, and 95\% Student-$t$ confidence intervals. Pareto relations use repeated-mean point estimates and are not significance tests.

Results are separated by protocol because one-shot search fitness, repeated training validation, and held-out validation answer different questions.

\subsection{Three-Seed Search: Provisional Fronts}
Across seeds 42, 123, and 999, the optimizer evaluates 216 candidates plus nine per-seed baseline references. This corresponds to 675 workload executions and 16,200 completed Pods. The one-shot combined fronts contain two, two, and one unique objective points, respectively; no baseline survives those provisional fronts. Five optimizer policies are therefore selected as validation finalists.

These observations alone look favorable to search, but each candidate was measured only once per workload. Exact objective coordinates also fail to recur across seeds. We therefore make no improvement claim from this stage; its purpose is finalist generation.

\subsection{Repeated Finalist Validation}
Repeated validation evaluates eight policies on three workloads with ten repetitions each: 240 policy--workload observations and 5,760 completed Pods. The repeated-mean Pareto front collapses to two hand-designed baselines. Balanced is the pending-best point at $(1.251855,3.6667)$ and consolidation-heavy is the node-best point at $(1.264390,3.0000)$. Their pooled 95\% latency intervals are $[1.199451,1.304258]$ and $[1.205896,1.322885]$ s. Thus, consolidation reduces mean occupied workers by 18.18\% for a 1.00\% point-estimate latency increase, but the overlapping intervals preclude a statistical-superiority statement.

None of the five optimizer finalists remains nondominated. The closest, \texttt{optimizer-s999-gen-03-002}, also uses three workers but has mean p95 $1.265704$ s, only $0.001313$ s above consolidation-heavy. That difference is much smaller than run variation. Figure~\ref{fig:repeated-validation} shows that every finalist is weakly behind one of the two baseline front points. With only two unique front points, no strict interior knee exists.

\subsection{Independent Holdout}
The holdout contains 30 observations and 720 completed Pods. All three baselines lie on the point-estimate Pareto front (Table~\ref{tab:holdout}). Spread-heavy is pending-best and uses four workers; consolidation-heavy uses two; balanced lies between them. Relative to spread-heavy, balanced reduces occupied workers by 25\% for a 0.60\% point-estimate latency increase. Consolidation-heavy halves occupied workers for a 4.11\% increase. The normalized line-distance rule, pre-specified and committed before execution, selects balanced as the interior knee.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The strongest result is methodological. A one-shot evaluator made every seed appear to improve upon the baselines, yet shared repeated validation removed every optimizer finalist. The search was functioning as implemented; its fitness estimate was insufficiently robust. Re-evaluation is therefore not a cosmetic reporting step but part of the optimization design for live systems. Future search should use repeated or adaptive resampling during selection, confidence-aware dominance, or a two-stage low-cost screening and high-confidence promotion protocol.

The holdout establishes a real policy tradeoff but not statistically distinct latency means. Its confidence intervals overlap, while occupied-worker counts and requested-load variance separate strongly. The responsible interpretation is that placement character changes reliably and latency point estimates move modestly. Larger repeated samples would be required to resolve those latency differences.

Each workload run contains only 24 Pods. With that sample size, the linearly interpolated p95 lies between the 22nd and 23rd ordered latency observations and is therefore sensitive to perturbations in only a few upper-tail samples. Repetition and interval reporting expose, but do not eliminate, this limitation.

External validity is limited. kind preserves the Kubernetes API, rollout, and binding path, but all four workers are homogeneous containers on one host. Workloads are synthetic, short, request-driven, and run without unrelated background demand or autoscaling. Maximum workload-occupied workers is not active infrastructure cost; requested-load variance is not measured CPU or memory utilization. Trace-derived workloads, heterogeneous workers, longer durations, and multi-host clusters remain future work.

The default kube-scheduler comparison also has a strict boundary. Common completion and placement metrics show that the default profile spreads these workloads across four workers, but its event-driven implementation does not emit the custom scheduler's decision annotation. A direct latency ranking would therefore mix timestamp sources and architectures. A future comparison could instrument a scheduler-framework plugin or use a shared tracing point.

Finally, optimizer hyperparameters were fixed rather than tuned, and the holdout contains only baselines because finalist selection was determined before holdout execution. Consequently, the study does not establish optimizer generalization, universal policy ordering, or superiority over Kubernetes defaults. It does establish a reproducible negative result and a workload-dependent policy tradeoff on a real control-plane path.

\section{Conclusion}
We built a versioned, parameterized scheduler and coupled multi-objective evolutionary search to real Kubernetes rollout, replay, measurement, and binding operations. The V2 representation removes scale redundancy and replaces a dependent packing feature with explicit balance and consolidation signals.

Three search seeds generated promising one-shot Pareto candidates, but none of the five finalists remained nondominated under repeated-mean evaluation. This negative result is the central lesson: live-cluster policy search can optimize measurement noise unless uncertainty is included in evaluation and selection. Separately, the independent holdout produced a point-estimate baseline front with three points spanning four, three, and two occupied workers, with balanced selected as the geometric knee. Thus, policy weights materially alter placement, but the best tradeoff remains workload-dependent.

The project demonstrates both why live evaluation matters and why it is not enough by itself. Reproducible manifests, repeated validation, held-out workloads, timestamp-source separation, and conservative claims are necessary parts of scheduler optimization. Code, workloads, commands, manifests, and raw results are available at \url{https://github.com/ismailtrm/evo-sched}.
